1. What This Simulator Is About (Scenario & People Affected)

The Problem: Our school uses a Student Learning Performance Monitoring System (SLPMS) to spot students who need extra help based on quiz scores and missed deadlines. However, setting strict rules can unintentionally punish students who have weak internet, force unnecessary travel to campus, invade student privacy, or bury teachers in paperwork.

People Affected: (1) Students (especially those without reliable home internet who must commute or submit late), (2) Teachers (who must check student appeals and give extra tutoring), (3) School Leaders (who must make sure grading rules are fair and easy to explain), and (4) The Local Environment (affected by daily student commuting emissions and printed paper waste).

    How Choices Affect Each Other (5 Interactions)

    • Rule 1 (Tutoring × Data) Full Tracking adds +15 Fairness ONLY when Tutoring is "By Need": Extra activity logs help teachers tell who truly needs help versus who just had slow Wi-Fi. Under strict deadlines, extra tracking just spies on students without helping them (Selwyn, 2019).
    • Rule 2 (Appeals × Reasons) Reviewing Every Appeal adds +10 Trust ONLY when Reasons are Clear: Teachers and students cannot fairly check or challenge a flag if the grading formula is hidden (Selbst et al., 2019).
    • Rule 3 (Appeals × Tutoring) Reviewing Every Appeal drops Teacher Workload by an extra -15 under Strict Deadlines: Strict late penalties cause tons of student appeals after power or signal outages, exhausting teachers (ACM Code of Ethics §1.2).
    • Rule 4 (Data × Reasons) Full Tracking drops Privacy by an extra -15 when Reasons are Hidden: Recording keystrokes and location without telling students how that data is used breaks basic privacy rules (Philippine Data Privacy Act, RA 10173).
    • Rule 5 (Travel × Tutoring) Online Submission First loses -15 Fairness under Strict Deadlines: Requiring online submission while enforcing strict late penalties hurts rural or low-income students who lack stable home data (UNESCO, 2021).
    What We Assumed
    • Submitting online from home cuts down daily jeepney/bus rides, saving fuel and paper.
    • Not every student has fast home internet, so flexible online/offline options improve fairness.
    • Teachers have limited work hours; more appeals and paper grading leave less time for teaching.
    Limits of This Simulator
    • The 0–100 scores show trade-offs between values, not exact liters of fuel or exact student grades.
    • It does not measure students who live within walking distance of school.
    • It assumes school budgets and staffing stay fixed during the semester.

    5. References & Guidelines Used

    1. ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018): Rules 1.1 (Help society and human wellbeing), 1.4 (Be fair and avoid discrimination), and 1.6 (Respect privacy).
    2. Republic Act No. 10173 (Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012): Section 11 — Schools must only collect student data that is necessary and clearly explained.
    3. UNESCO (2021): Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence — Protecting the environment, ensuring human oversight, and avoiding harm to disadvantaged learners.
    4. Selbst, A. D., et al. (2019): Fairness and Abstraction in Sociotechnical Systems. ACM FAT* '19, 59–68 (Why transparent rules matter for fair appeals).
    5. Selwyn, N. (2019): Should Robots Replace Teachers? AI and the Future of Education. Polity Press (Risks of heavy student surveillance in schools).
    6. Caird, S., Lane, A., Swithenby, E., Roy, R., & Potter, S. (2015): Design of higher education courses and the carbon impacts of student travel. International Journal of Sustainability in Higher Education, 16(1), 89–106 (How online submission reduces student commuting emissions).
